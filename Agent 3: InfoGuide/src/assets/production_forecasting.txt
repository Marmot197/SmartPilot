Foresight Agent
Abstract— I. INTRODUCTION 
A. Contextual Background 
In the era of Industry 4.0, real-time forecasting has become an essential component of intelligent manufacturing systems. Accurate and timely prediction of process outcomes not only supports proactive decision-making but also enables consistent quality control, optimized resource utilization, and reduced operational downtime. This is particularly critical in highvariability batch processes such as industrial fermentation, where complex physicochemical interactions and raw material heterogeneity significantly affect final product quality. The increasing availability of Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) sensor data offers new opportunities for developing robust data-driven forecasting models to support such complex industrial ecosystems. 
B. Challenges 
Despite these opportunities, several challenges persist. Industrial fermentation processes—such as those used in yeast production for food-grade extracts—exhibit nonlinear, multivariate dynamics influenced by a combination of manually controlled parameters, fluctuating raw material properties, and time-sensitive enzymatic reactions. Modeling these processes requires not only capturing temporal dependencies across noisy, highdimensional sensor streams but also accommodating domain-specific operational constraints and rare, context-sensitive anomalies. Traditional statistical models and black-box machine learning methods often fall short in such settings due to their inability to generalize under distributional shifts or provide actionable insights to process operators. 
C. Existing Solutions 
Conventional forecasting methods such as AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), while interpretable, are often inadequate for capturing nonlinear dependencies and multivariate correlations in sensor-rich environments. On the other hand, deep learning models—especially Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks—have demonstrated substantial improvements in modeling sequential industrial data. However, standard LSTM architectures tend to treat all inputs with equal representational priority, ignoring domain knowledge that could inform variable relevance and improve model generalization in data-sparse or volatile regions. Additionally, these models often lack interpretability, posing challenges for deployment in regulated industrial settings where explainability and operator trust are paramount. 
D. Study Objectives 
To address these gaps, this study presents the SmartPilot Foresight Agent, an interpretable and knowledge-aware forecasting framework designed for industrial fermentation processes. Using a real-world dataset from a Vegemite yeast production facility, we evaluate and compare three forecasting approaches: • Standard LSTM: A baseline deep learning model trained on all available variables without domain-informed selection. • Knowledge-Infused LSTM: An enhanced variant that incorporates expert-identified critical process variables to guide the model’s attention and improve contextual relevance. • Knowledge-Infused XGBoost: A nonsequential, tree-based regression model that leverages lag features and domain knowledge for interpretable and efficient forecasting. Through systematic benchmarking, we demonstrate that infusing domain knowledge significantly improves predictive accuracy, model robustness, and interpretability. Our results highlight the practical value of combining symbolic reasoning and machine learning for high-precision industrial forecasting and lay the groundwork for future integration into real-time process control and decision support systems.

III. RELATED WORK 
Forecasting and predictive analytics in industrial settings have gained increasing relevance with the rise of Industry 4.0 and smart manufacturing systems. Various methods have been explored for time series forecasting, predictive maintenance, and demand estimation using data from complex industrial processes. Among these, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have demonstrated notable success in capturing temporal dependencies, handling nonlinearity, and enabling robust predictions over sequential sensor data. Additionally, hybrid modeling approaches that combine statistical models, machine learning, and domain knowledge have emerged as powerful tools for enhancing forecasting reliability and interpretability. This section reviews prior work in three categories: (A) LSTM in Industrial Forecasting, (B) Knowledge-Infused Models, and (C) XGBoost Applications. 
A. LSTM in Industrial Forecasting 
LSTM-based models have become prominent in industrial time series forecasting due to their ability to capture long-term dependencies and nonlinear patterns in sensor, load, and operational data. Several studies have demonstrated the versatility of LSTM architectures across different industrial domains. The work by Zhang et al. on LSTM-Based Analysis of Industrial IoT Equipments [1] presents a deep neural model that predicts equipment running status using sensor data from 33 channels of a power station pump. Their method integrates feature engineering and hyperparameter optimization to outperform traditional ARIMA models in terms of root mean square error (RMSE). Similarly, Tan et al. propose a hybrid ensemble LSTM model for ultra-short-term power demand forecasting [2]. By combining bagging, boosting, and random subspace techniques along with a peakaware loss function, their approach demonstrates superior robustness and forecasting accuracy on both synthetic and real-world power datasets. In the energy sector, Sagheer et al. explore petroleum production forecasting using a deep LSTM (DLSTM) network optimized via genetic algorithms [3]. Their DLSTM significantly outperforms traditional and soft computing methods across two real oilfield datasets, showing the model’s capability in capturing complex stochastic dependencies. From the supply chain perspective, research on applying LSTM networks to forecast furniture sales based on time series of daily transaction data [4]. Emphasizing stationarity and seasonal adjustments, their model learns long-term sales trends and enhances supply chain decision-making. Expanding on demand forecasting, some work presents a multi-layer LSTM model that automatically identifies optimal configurations through grid search [5]. When tested on furniture industry data, their model outperforms ARIMA, KNN, SVM, and even single-layer LSTM architectures, proving the advantage of deeper LSTM stacks in capturing demand fluctuations. [6] focus on predictive maintenance through LSTM models trained on three years of industrial press sensor data. Their model forecasts equipment behavior up to a month in advance, enabling more informed and cost-effective maintenance scheduling. Finally, Mejri et al. compare ARIMA and LSTM for forecasting demand in detergent manufacturing [7]. LSTM models achieve a significantly lower prediction error (22%) compared to ARIMA (34%), highlighting the superiority of deep learning for medium- and long-term demand estimation under uncertainty. Together, these works establish the effectiveness and adaptability of LSTM-based models across industrial contexts—from equipment health monitoring and maintenance to load forecasting and supply chain optimization. However, despite their predictive strength, such models often lack semantic interpretability, motivating our integration of symbolic reasoning and knowledge representation within the SmartPilot Foresight Agent. 
B. XGBoost Applications 
Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) has emerged as an effective ensemble learning technique in industrial applications, particularly for its robustness, scalability, and strong performance with structured tabular data. It has been extensively adopted for predictive tasks involving manufacturing quality, process optimization, and production forecasting where both accuracy and interpretability are crucial.In the context of machining operations, Chakraborty and Bhattacharya demonstrate the use of XGBoost as a predictive tool for forecasting surface roughness and material removal rate in CNC turning processes [8]. Their approach explores over 24,600 hyperparameter configurations, with the final model exhibiting superior prediction accuracy validated by multiple statistical error estimators. This highlights XGBoost’s capability in modeling complex physical interactions in subtractive manufacturing. To address challenges in aluminum alloy casting, particularly under conditions of sparse labeled data, Wang et al. propose a self-training framework with an enhanced XGBoost objective function [9]. Their model penalizes misclassification of unlabeled samples by introducing a regularization term that captures pseudolabel uncertainty. This hybrid learning setup achieves 93.2% accuracy in quality prediction tasks across multiple casting types and proves valuable for rapid deployment in industrial settings with limited supervision. In robotic manufacturing, another study applies a novel k-fold-XGBoost model to predict material removal in belt grinding of Inconel 718 using features derived from acoustic emissions [10]. By integrating signal denoising via wavelet decomposition and Fourier transformation, their model achieves a MAPE of just 4.37%, outperforming existing baselines such as random forests and support vector regressors. The findings support the effectiveness of XGBoost in learning from sensor-rich, noisy environments to model complex removal dynamics. Together, these works reinforce the applicability of XGBoost in diverse industrial contexts—ranging from machining and casting to robotic finishing—where predictive accuracy, flexibility in handling small or noisy datasets, and efficient learning are essential. 
C. Knowledge-Infused Models 
While deep learning methods such as LSTMs and ensemble models have shown remarkable success in industrial forecasting, they often operate as black boxes with limited interpretability, data efficiency, or contextual awareness. This has led to the emergence of KnowledgeInfused Learning (KiL), which seeks to integrate structured domain knowledge with statistical learning to enhance both predictive performance and trustworthiness. These hybrid models—also known as neuro-symbolic systems—bridge the gap between symbolic reasoning and data-driven AI, offering improvements in generalization, explainability, and safety. The continuum of knowledge infusion strategies spans from shallow (embedding symbolic cues), to semi-deep (training constraints), to deep (injecting ontologies or causal structures into latent spaces) [11]. This framework bridges the gap between symbolic and neural AI, especially valuable in low-data and safetycritical settings. Structured knowledge (e.g., rules, graphs) can improves robustness, explainability, and context-awareness [12]. These qualities are essential in industrial systems, where predictions must be traceable, aligned with domain logic, and user-understandable. Together, these works underscore the relevance of knowledge-infused modeling for systems, where AI predictions are not only required to be accurate but also aligned with operational context, comprehensible to stakeholders, and safe for deployment in industrial environments. The Foresight Agent in SmartPilot builds on these principles by incorporating structured process ontologies and sensor semantics into forecasting and explanation tasks, enabling more trustworthy and context-aware decision support.

The manufacturing process involved monitoring evaporator operations, where machine settings such as flow rate, pressure, and feed rates were manually or semi-automatically adjusted to maintain product quality within strict specification limits. The dataset incorporates critical process variables such as VMBX - Auto Yield, VMBX - Auto Feed, VYA - Papain added, VYA - Final Volume, VFX - Feed SS, and VMBX - Res IS/TS (%), among others. These variables are directly related to key control points in the evaporation process, influencing evaporation rates, solids concentration, enzymatic activity, and final product consistency [13]. Comprehensive preprocessing was essential to ensure data integrity and facilitate effective model training. Missing values, which arose primarily due to occasional sensor faults or downtime events, were treated using forwardfill imputation methods that preserved the temporal sequence of production dynamics. For more persistent missing segments, domaininformed interpolation techniques were applied when physically plausible; otherwise, the affected variables were excluded to prevent introducing artifacts. Outlier detection was performed using a combination of statistical techniques (e.g., interquartile range analysis) and expert validation to eliminate aberrant readings caused by sensor drift, calibration errors, or production anomalies. Chronological alignment checks ensured the correct sequencing of events across production runs, a crucial requirement for recurrent neural network models such as Long ShortTerm Memory (LSTM) networks. Feature scaling was performed using MinMax normalization, rescaling all numerical variables into the [0, 1] interval. This approach was selected over z-score standardization due to the enhanced convergence properties observed during preliminary LSTM model tuning, consistent with best practices in time-series forecasting where the amplitude of signals influences memory cell dynamics [14]. To augment temporal feature richness, additional lag features and rolling mean statistics were engineered for selected variables, capturing short-term trends and smoothing stochastic fluctuations inherent to industrial fermentation systems. These engineered features enhanced the models’ ability to recognize process states evolving over time, thereby improving forecast accuracy. A pivotal distinction between the standard and knowledge-infused LSTM models lies in the strategic prioritization of input features. For the standard Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model, the complete set of available variables was utilized without explicit prioritization, allowing the network to learn feature relevance autonomously. Conversely, for the knowledge-infused LSTM model, domain expertise was systematically incorporated to enrich the input representation. After thorough consultations and operational analyses, six variables were identified as crucial: VMBX - Auto Yield, VMBX - Auto Feed, VYA - Papain added, VYA - Final Volume, VFX - Feed SS, and VMBX - Res IS/TS (%) [13], [14]. These variables reflect key control levers for achieving targeted evaporation and concentration dynamics critical to yeast paste production quality. By integrating such expert-selected features, the knowledge-infused LSTM model sought to allocate greater representational capacity to physiochemically significant variables, thereby enhancing forecasting precision and supporting interpretable, domain-aligned model behavior.

C. Evaluation Metrics and Validation 
a) Performance Metrics: To assess the predictive accuracy and generalization ability of the proposed models, three standard regression evaluation metrics were employed: Mean Squared Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R2 ). Each metric offers complementary insights into model performance. MSE is defined as: MSE = 1 n Xn i=1 (yi − yˆi) 2 , where yi denotes the observed value, yˆi is the predicted value, and n is the number of predictions. MSE penalizes larger errors more heavily and is sensitive to outliers. MAE, on the other hand, is given by: MAE = 1 n Xn i=1 |yi − yˆi | , which measures the average absolute deviation between predictions and observations, providing a more interpretable measure of typical prediction error. The coefficient of determination (R2 ) is used to evaluate the proportion of variance in the observed variable that is predictable from the input features: R 2 = 1 − Pn i=1(yi − yˆi) 2 Pn i=1(yi − y¯) 2 , where y¯ is the mean of the observed values. An R2 value closer to 1 indicates a better model fit, whereas a value near or below zero suggests poor predictive performance. These metrics were computed for each model across the forecasting horizon to enable consistent comparison between architectures. 
b) Validation Strategy: To ensure rigorous and unbiased model evaluation, a systematic data partitioning scheme was employed. The complete dataset was chronologically ordered and split into training, validation, and test sets using a 70:15:15 ratio. The training set was used for model fitting, while the validation set guided hyperparameter tuning and early stopping procedures. The test set, held out entirely from training, was used for final performance reporting. For the LSTM-based models, sequencebased splitting was preserved to maintain temporal consistency and avoid information leakage across time steps. Input sequences were generated using a sliding window approach with a fixed look-back length of 30 timesteps. Each sequence was paired with a corresponding ground truth value at the prediction horizon. For the XGBoost model, input vectors were created using flattened lagged features derived from the same look-back window. A separate regressor was trained for each target output, and performance was aggregated across all three outputs. No cross-validation was used due to the sequential nature of the data, which prohibits random shuffling. However, experiments were repeated across multiple initialization seeds to ensure robustness, and standard deviation of performance metrics was recorded where applicable. All evaluations were conducted consistently across models using the same test partition to enable fair performance comparison in Section V-A.

b) Practical Implications: From a production standpoint, accurate forecasting of yeast output is essential for maintaining target paste quality and minimizing reprocessing cycles. The results of this study suggest that hybrid approaches combining domain expertise with advanced machine learning architectures can significantly enhance production planning. The XGBoost model, in particular, provides interpretable predictions with low computational overhead, making it well-suited for integration into decision support systems within real-time manufacturing environments. The use of domain-informed features such as Papain added and Feed SS—both of which directly influence evaporation kinetics—also opens opportunities for feedback control strategies. By embedding these models into supervisory systems, operators could anticipate deviations from target solids concentrations and adjust machine settings proactively, thereby improving product quality consistency and reducing energy and material waste. c) Limitations: While the results are promising, several limitations warrant consideration. First, all models were evaluated on a single historical dataset collected under specific process configurations. Although the dataset captures a representative sample of operational behavior, generalizability to other fermentation systems or production lines remains to be validated. Second, the current models operate at fixed time intervals without dynamically adjusting to varying batch durations or sensor update rates, which may affect temporal resolution and forecasting latency in real-time applications. Moreover, while XGBoost provided superior performance, its reliance on static input vectors limits its applicability to multi-horizon forecasting unless explicitly restructured for iterative prediction. LSTM-based models, while naturally suited for such tasks, are more computationally intensive and less interpretable. Future work should explore ensemble strategies that combine temporal modeling with tree-based decision support to balance accuracy, interpretability, and runtime efficiency. 
VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
a) Summary of Findings: This study presents a comparative evaluation of three datadriven models—Standard LSTM, KnowledgeInfused LSTM, and Knowledge-Infused XGBoost—for forecasting yeast production in an industrial fermentation context. By leveraging real-world process data and incorporating domain knowledge, the study demonstrates how model performance can be significantly enhanced through targeted feature selection. The Standard LSTM model, while capable of modeling temporal dependencies, showed limited predictive accuracy due to its reliance on unfiltered multivariate inputs. In contrast, the Knowledge-Infused LSTM improved generalization by focusing on six key variables with known influence on product quality, as identified by process experts. The KnowledgeInfused XGBoost model, benefiting from both structured feature engineering and an inherently interpretable tree-based framework, consistently achieved superior results across all three target outputs. Quantitatively, the knowledge-infused models yielded substantial reductions in error metrics and improvements in R2 scores compared to the baseline. These results highlight the value of aligning machine learning models with domain insights in high-variability industrial environments. 
b) Future Directions: Future work should explore hybrid modeling approaches that combine the temporal representation power of LSTM architectures with the interpretability and robustness of gradient-boosted decision trees. Such ensemble systems may offer a balanced trade-off between accuracy, explainability, and computational efficiency. In addition, extending the models to accommodate real-time streaming data and dynamic retraining could enable adaptive forecasting under process drift and operational variability. Broadening the scope of prediction to include upstream and downstream stages of the production pipeline would also support a more integrated approach to process optimization. Moreover, integrating explainability tools such as SHAP values or attention mechanisms would enhance transparency and user confidence in model predictions. Finally, future deployment efforts should prioritize seamless integration into industrial control systems, accompanied by usability assessments and operator feedback, to ensure practical utility and long-term adoption.
